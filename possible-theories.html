Possible theories

There are 3 prevailing theories:

  1. Remittances: literature shows that districts with high emigrants have low FLFPRs; and districts with relatively low share in remittances income have a higher female labour force participation rate.
    1. The most conclusive study is by Khan & Valatheeswaran (2016) titled “International Migration, Remittances and Labour Force Participation of Left-behind Family Members: A study of Kerala”. This study found that left-behind members reduce their labour supply in response to an international migrant member in the household. The effect of international migration varies in magnitude among male and female members. The left-behind male members decrease their supply in the labour market in response to the migration of a household member by 24 per cent, while left-behind female members and left-behind wives decrease their labour force participation by 46 and 49 per cent, respectively.
    2. However, the study is based on NSS 2011 unemployment data & Kerala Migration Survey 2010. An update to this study is required, with the availability of PLFS 2023-24 and Kerala Migration Survey 2023 data readily available.
  2. Marriage: A 2021 study by CSES shows that marriage affects women's employment due to relocation, caregiving burden and family pressures. This is a pan-India explanation, caused by the income effect of combined married households and the prevalence of women in informal, unpaid caregiving roles, as shown in MOSPI’s Time Use Survey 2024. The lack of wanting to relocate for a job is another factor which affects female employment, though primary quantitative data on this is lacking.
    1. However, this study is based on PLFS 2018-19 and a 2020 village level study data (I couldn’t find a link to it). Given that women’s FLFP has consistently increased since 2020-21, the changing nature of women’s employment must be re-examined.

  1. Choice of higher education & exam preparation:
    1. A 2015-16 Kerala State Planning Board study cites the effect of more women pursuing arts in higher education as a factor affecting women’s absorption into employment. However the recent 2022-23 All India Survey on Higher Education (AISHE) doesn’t have data splits between men and women pursuing arts/science at undergraduate & postgraduate levels, by state, which makes it difficult to corroborate this.
    2. There is also a prevailing theory that the affinity for preparation of government jobs for women contributes to unemployment. But the CSE study points out that only 12 per cent of unemployed women and 8 per cent of unemployed men expressed a preference for government jobs.

Conclusion

Kerala presents a development paradox: despite decades of progressive social indicators, near-universal literacy, and female higher education enrolment reaching near-parity, the state continues to grapple with low female labour force participation. In recent years, post-pandemic economic restructuring, large-scale youth migration, and a rising informal caregiving burden are fundamentally reshaping the domestic sphere. The current literature lacks up-to-date empirical evidence on how these shifting realities affect the ‘missing middle’: the substantial cohort of educated, working-age women who remain outside the active labor force. Addressing this gap is necessary for a vision for the working women of Kerala.
