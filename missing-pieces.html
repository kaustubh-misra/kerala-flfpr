The missing pieces

Is it education? Across states, the share of young women in higher education tells us almost nothing about how many women are in the labour force.

Kerala women have high college Gross Enrolment Rates (47.8%). Logic suggests that rising higher education should automatically expand female labour force participation (FLFPR). So why is it low?

In India, however, this intuition breaks down entirely. Across Indian states, the statistical correlation between the College Gross Enrolment Ratio (GER) and FLFPR is effectively non-existent or slightly negative (r=-0.05) for PLFS & AISHE 2021-22 data. Yes it is only one year and a longitudinal correlation is needed, but it still gives us a picture of the reality. States with low college GERs have much higher female participation than tertiary education leaders like Chandigarh and Kerala.

Development economics tries to explain this as the "Indian Education-Employment Paradox." Stephan Klasen & Janneke Pieters in their landmark paper What Explains the Stagnation of Female Labour Force Participation in Urban India? show that education and employment have an inverse relationship to some extent.

Female labor force participation traces a distinct U-shaped curve across educational tiers: at the base, uneducated women enter the workforce out of basic survival necessity, working primarily as unpaid family farm helpers or casual manual laborers. Moving into the trough of the U, as women complete higher secondary or general undergraduate degrees (such as a BA, B.Com, or BSc), their reservation wages and social expectations rise, leading them to withdraw from manual, informal, or agricultural labor even as the economy fails to generate sufficient white-collar, non-manual jobs to absorb them. It is only along the right arm of the curve that participation ticks upward again, driven by elite graduates with specialized technical or postgraduate credentials (such as an MBBS or top-tier MBA) whose earning potential is finally high enough to offset childcare costs.

So what is causing this missing middle, the educated women in Kerala who are out of the workforce?

Sources and method

Sources

Female GER: Ministry of Education, All India Survey on Higher Education (AISHE) 2021-22. Women enrolled in higher education as a share of the female population aged 18–23.

Female LFPR: Ministry of Statistics and Programme Implementation (MoSPI), Periodic Labour Force Survey (PLFS), July 2021 – June 2022. Female, age 15+, rural + urban, usual status (ps+ss). Retrieved from the MoSPI eSankhyiki API (PLFS indicator 1) on 26 October 2026.

Agrarian tiers as on the previous page (agriculture share of GSVA, 2023-24).

Method

Correlations are computed across the 36 states and UTs, one value each (All India is not included). Pearson's r measures a straight-line relationship, and Spearman's ρ compares the rank orders. p-values are two-sided. The dashed trend line is an ordinary least-squares fit of female LFPR on female GER. Computed with SciPy in build_ger_data.py.

Every state and UT counts equally, so Lakshadweep counts as much as Uttar Pradesh. A correlation across states also can't say anything about whether educated women, as individuals, are more or less likely to work.

GER counts students where they study, but measures them against the population of the state they study in. Education hubs like Chandigarh, Puducherry and Delhi are pushed up by students from other states. Lakshadweep (1.2%) is pushed down because most of its students go to the mainland. Leaving out Lakshadweep gives r = , which is still far from significant.

The two measures cover different age groups: GER covers ages 18–23, and LFPR covers every woman aged 15 and over.
